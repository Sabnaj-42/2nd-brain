# Analytical Writing -- "Analyze an Issue" Task

The current (since Sep 22, 2023) GRE has **one** essay: the **"Analyze an Issue"** task, **30
minutes**. You write a response to a general-interest opinion prompt, taking and defending a
position. Scored 0-6 in half-point increments; reported separately from V/Q.

---

## What it tests
- Articulating a clear position and supporting it with **relevant reasons and examples**.
- Organizing ideas into a **coherent, well-focused** essay.
- Controlling **standard written English** (grammar, sentence variety, mechanics).

It is NOT about having the "right" opinion -- it's about reasoned, well-supported argument.

---

## Task format (typical prompt)

> "Universities should require every student to take a variety of courses outside the student's
> field of study."
>
> Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the
> recommendation and explain your reasoning for the position you take.

---

## Essay template (5 paragraphs, ~500-600 words)

**Paragraph 1 -- Thesis (3-4 sentences)**
- Restate the issue in your own words.
- State your position clearly (agree / disagree / qualified agreement).
- Preview your 2-3 main reasons.

**Paragraphs 2-3 (and 4) -- Body (one reason each)**
- Topic sentence = the reason.
- Develop with a **specific example** (history, current events, science, personal experience,
  hypothetical scenario). Specific > vague.
- Tie the example back to the thesis explicitly.

**Counterargument (can be its own short paragraph or woven in)**
- Acknowledge the strongest opposing view, then **rebut** it. This adds depth and nuance (graders
  reward complexity).

**Final paragraph -- Conclusion (2-3 sentences)**
- Restate thesis in fresh words; summarize reasoning briefly; end with a forward-looking or
  synthesizing sentence. Avoid introducing new arguments here.

---

## Scoring rubric (what earns a 5-6)

- **Insightful position** that addresses the complexity of the issue.
- **Compelling reasons and/or persuasive examples** -- concrete, relevant, well-chosen.
- **Clear, logical organization** with smooth transitions; focused on the main idea throughout.
- **Strong control of language** -- varied sentence structure, precise vocabulary, few errors.
- Acknowledges **nuance/counterarguments**.

**What sinks scores (1-3):** vague generalities, no specific examples, off-topic, disorganized,
frequent grammar/mechanics errors, one-sided without nuance.

---

## 30-minute pacing

| Time | Task |
|---|---|
| 0-3 min | Read prompt; brainstorm position + 2-3 reasons + examples; jot a quick outline. |
| 3-26 min | Write the essay (intro, 2-3 body paragraphs, counterpoint, conclusion). |
| 26-30 min | Proofread -- fix grammar, sharpen topic sentences, cut repetition. |

> Planning for 2-3 minutes is the single biggest score-booster. Don't start typing immediately.

## Sample essay (abbreviated, for the prompt above)

**Position: Qualified agreement.**

A liberal-arts breadth requirement can enrich students, but a rigid, one-size-fits-all mandate risks
diluting depth and burdening those in highly technical fields. A flexible version is best.

First, exposure outside one's discipline builds versatile thinking. A computer scientist who studies
ethics is better equipped to reason about algorithmic bias, and a philosopher who learns statistics
can evaluate evidence more rigorously. Such crossover often sparks innovation at the intersections
of fields. Second, breadth helps students who are unsure of their path discover unexpected interests,
as many career trajectories originate in a serendipitous elective.

However, a blanket requirement can be counterproductive. In accredited engineering or pre-medical
programs, curricula are already packed with essential technical coursework; forcing additional
non-major courses may delay graduation or force students to drop meaningful electives. Moreover,
mandated breadth can foster superficial engagement -- students may treat the requirement as a box to
check rather than an opportunity to learn.

The strongest policy therefore balances the two: require *some* exposure but allow choice, perhaps
through thematic clusters (e.g., "society and technology") rather than arbitrary distribution rules.
This preserves discovery without sacrificing depth.

In sum, while intellectual breadth is valuable, universities should pursue it through flexible
structures rather than rigid mandates, honoring both exploration and the demands of specialized
fields.

## Practice tips

- ETS publishes the full **"Analyze an Issue" topic pool** -- practice brainstorming outlines for
  several prompts (you don't need to fully write each). Search "ETS GRE issue topic pool".
- Write 1 untimed essay first (focus on structure), then graduate to strictly **30-min timed**.
- Self-score with the official rubric; ideally get feedback from a strong writer.
- Keep a personal list of versatile example "stories" (a few historical, scientific, technological,
  and personal anecdotes that adapt to many prompts).
