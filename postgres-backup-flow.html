KubeDB · PostgreSQL

Postgres Backup & Restore, End to End

Four repositories, one KubeStash control plane, and three completely separate routes from a database to a bucket. The thing worth holding onto: KubeDB runs two cadences at once — scheduled jobs that wake, copy, and die, and one always-on process that never stops — and almost every confusing behaviour in this system comes from the seam between them.

  • Scheduled — cron-driven, ephemeral Job
  • Continuous — always-on sidekick
  • Gate — blocks until satisfied
01

Who owns what

One operator decides, three separate binaries do the copying.

The Postgres operator never moves a byte of backup data. Its entire job is to translate a user's intent into resources other components act on — then wait, and gate. The actual copying is done by short-lived Jobs it does not create and a sidekick pod it does not run.

Worth noticing

The archiver image appears twice in the lifecycle — once as the long-lived WAL pusher during backup, and once as a short-lived pod during restore that runs /scripts/restore.sh and replays WAL. Same image, opposite direction. It is the only component that both writes to and reads from the bucket.

02

Where KubeStash plugs in

Three independent chains that meet inside one Job.

KubeStash is not one graph, it is three, and they only touch at the moment a Job is built. One chain says where the bytes go, one says when to run, one says what binary to run. Understanding which chain a problem lives in is most of debugging this system.

The Function image is a template, not a literal: postgres-restic-plugin:v0.29.0_${DB_VERSION}. ${DB_VERSION} is substituted from the backup target's version, and the Function declares which values it accepts (12.17, 14.10, 16.4, 17.2, 18.2). A target whose version isn't on that list produces an image tag that doesn't exist, and the failure surfaces as ImagePullBackOff rather than anything that mentions versions.

03

Which backup is taken when

The startup order, and the one gate that holds everything up.

Adding spec.archiver to a Postgres flips two environment variables with different jobs. WAL_BACKUP_TYPE=WALG makes the init scripts write a real archive_command; ARCHIVER_ENABLED creates the directories. Postgres therefore begins archiving early — but the process that ships those segments off the node does not start until a full backup has already succeeded.

The failure this prevents

If the sidekick started first and the full backup later failed, you would hold a pile of WAL with no base to replay it onto — useless bytes that still cost money. The gate guarantees the invariant every PITR system depends on: there is always a base backup older than the oldest WAL segment you kept.

04

How the bytes reach the bucket

Four routes that share nothing — not a tool, not a format, not a credential.

Nothing is staged on local disk on the way out. The two restic routes pipe their producer's stdout straight into restic backup --stdin, so a 400 GB base backup never needs 400 GB of scratch space. The WAL route is the exception, and deliberately so — see the next section for why.

A consequence worth planning for

Because the two chains are independent, retention is too. The archiver runs its own retention cron over the WAL prefix (logBackup.retentionPeriod), separate from the KubeStash RetentionPolicy that prunes restic snapshots. Set one without the other and you get either WAL you can't replay or base backups with nothing to replay onto them.

05

Why WAL takes two hops

Postgres never talks to object storage. That is the point.

06

How a point in time is resolved

Choosing the base is a sort, not a search.

Once the base is chosen, the target itself is handed to Postgres as a recovery parameter — recovery_target_lsn when an LSN is known, recovery_target_time otherwise. Postgres replays until it reaches the first commit past the target, stops before it, and promotes onto a new timeline. The stopping point is a transaction boundary, never a partial write.

07

Restore, stage by stage

Five stages, each gated on the one before it.

A restore is not one job. It is a sequence the operator drives from the outside, re-entering its reconcile loop after every stage and refusing to advance until the previous stage reports success. Nothing here runs concurrently by accident.

08

How the data lands in the pod

Three writers, one volume, strictly in order.

This is the part that surprises people: the restore never goes through the database. No psql, no network protocol, no import. Two separate pods mount the database's own PVC and write into it directly, at different mount paths, before Postgres is allowed to open it.

Why the base must be physical

WAL can only be replayed onto a byte-level copy — the LSNs in a segment refer to physical page positions. That is why the archiver's full backup is always pg_basebackup or a VolumeSnapshot and never pg_dumpall: a logical dump produces a different physical layout, and there is nothing for the WAL to attach to.
